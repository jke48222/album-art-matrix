The shared body

Bottom-weighted, because a framer would and a panel demands it

64.8 mm of cloth above and to the sides, 185 mm below. Framers deepen a lower margin so an image does not look like it is sliding out of its mount. Here it also has a job: the readout is the spare 64×64 panel standing below the aperture, and 160 mm of panel plus clearance sets the number. Under 175 and its top edge shows inside the picture window — which is exactly what the first render did.

The face is three layers and no sign standoffs at all: smoked acrylic 6.7 mm off the LEDs, a cloth-wrapped 3 mm mask sitting on it so the picture is recessed behind a crisp shadow line, and an alloy bezel standing in the opening. Losing the standoffs saves about $15 and four nervous holes drilled in acrylic.

A finding that overrode a choice

Flush and floating cannot happen

You picked both the deep shadow gap and flush mounting. The electronics need the 63.5 mm cavity behind the board whatever the face does, and the halo needs the body held off the wall to have anything to wash. True flush gives the light nowhere to go and turns the halo into a hairline. All four directions are therefore shadow-gap mounted on four 45 mm pads, with the strip on the outer face of the back panel.

Found by building it wrong first: the halo was originally inside the closed cavity, sealed in by the back panel, lighting nothing.

Studio proposal

4 · Nonet

Every version of this build so far, mine included, has been trying to hide that it is nine panels. It cannot be hidden. The shells butt at 0.2 mm and the pixel pitch breaks at every join, so a single continuous window puts a visible fault line across the artwork three times in each direction.

Making the grid explicit turns the one unfixable defect into the subject, and the 8 mm land falls exactly on the fault. It has a lineage worth standing in: the polyptych altarpiece is one image divided by structure, which is precisely the reliquary register you asked for; the nine-square grid runs through Albers and Agnes Martin; and the object then sits in the line from Rams' wall-mounted Braun system, the first of its kind, through Fukasawa's Muji player, whose whole idea is one honest gesture on a wall.

Off, it is the strongest of the four. Nothing about nine dark squares in a field of wool says screen. And it is the cheapest: nine small apertures in one sheet, one piece of acrylic behind all of them, and no 500 mm span of unsupported glass.

CheaperNone needed — this is the cheap one. Paint the aperture edges instead of buying bezel angle and it drops under $190 all in.

Colourways

Nonet in all five

The body is black in every one. Cloth is where the colour goes, because cloth reads as considered where paint reads as painted.

The halo

Thirty-six emitters, twelve colour zones

Three zones an edge, one to each panel, each the average of the artwork's own outer strip, lifted and saturated — an average is always duller than the picture it came from, and a dull halo just looks like a fault. The renders read the real face image, so those are the colours the wall would actually throw.

Each zone is split three ways so the wall gets a wash rather than three hot spots, which is what the first version produced.

The readout

The tenth panel, and what it costs you

The bonnet has three ports and every port must carry the same number of panels, so a tenth cannot chain on. It gets its own driver: the spare Pico 2 W, which already has the PIO for HUB75 and is currently only a bench colorimeter host. Twelve pixel rows show through the letterbox window as an amber level meter.

Two costs to be clear about. You lose your spare panel, which is the one thing standing between you and a dead tile. And it is a second firmware plus a link from the Pi, most simply UART over two wires.

Either way

Studies now, or one of them is the build

As studies. The plywood, steel and smoked-acrylic design with its three enclosures stays the plan of record and nothing in the assembly guide changes. Every alternate uses the same board, the same fifteen openings, the same electronics layout and the same 63.5 mm cavity, so any can be retrofitted later by making a new face. The board never comes off the wall.

As the build. Three things change and nothing else does. The shopping list gains cloth, a mask sheet, alloy and an encoder. The board is cut 609.6 × 729.8 instead of square and the steel 609.6 × 490. The glass section loses the four standoffs and gains the three-layer face. Wiring, openings, tile map, white balance and hanging are untouched.

That retrofit path is the reason to start simple: nothing bought this week is wasted by any of these.

What a render cannot settle

The Pico's HUB75 output has been established as possible in principle and never tried on one of these panels. The cloth is a shader, not a fabric: how a real weave takes the halo's colour at a grazing angle is not something a render answers. The magnet protrusion is 16.6 mm of published length less an assumed 6 mm of thread, and one ruler against a fridge door would confirm it. And the knob's detent count and the idle animation are software that does not exist yet.
