Two objects assembled out of the six that survived review, and a third that argues with all of them.

Six bodies were liked: Index and Soft Monument from the ChatGPT study, Console and Vitrine from the first alternates, the fully boxed enclosure, and the first walnut frame. They have less in common than they look. The useful work was separating what each one actually contributes from what it merely happens to be made of.

The knob can come back

Console had a knob and the second round removed it. That was right for the reason given and wrong for the reason assumed.

The Triple Bonnet uses every GPIO on the header except pins 27 and 28, and the assembly guide already spends those on the lux sensor. So an encoder on plain GPIO is genuinely impossible, and no rearranging fixes it.

An I2C encoder is a different question. Adafruit's seesaw rotary breakout sits at address 0x36 and the lux sensor at 0x10. Two devices, one bus, the same two pins, no second microcontroller, no second firmware, and nothing taken from the spare panel.

That is the whole argument for Console's control returning and Console's tenth-panel readout staying dropped.

Unchanged

Nine panels, 480 mm square, on their own magnets on the same steel skin, with the same fifteen openings and the same electrical layout. Every one of these is a new face on the wall already being built.

Dropped for good

The tenth-panel readout, which costs the spare tile. The four sign standoffs, because the glass now sits in the bezel's rebate.

Vitrine's recess is the strongest single idea in the six, because it is the only one that changes what the object is rather than what it looks like. The picture stops being on the wall and starts being inside something.

It had one real fault. A straight-sided well shadows a strip d · tan θ wide off the far side of the picture at any angle off axis, so 40 mm deep and 20 degrees off you lose 15 mm, about six pixels. Splaying the walls 11.3 degrees opens the mouth from 496 to 512 and gives back the first eleven degrees free. It costs 16 mm of width and one bevel on four strips.

The walls also shade the glass from room light, which is the job the smoked tint was doing alone. The well and the tint stop competing for the same problem.

Around that: the walnut frame's mitred body, Index's bright edge at the throat, Console's bottom weighting and its band, and the boxed enclosure's closed, vented, padded back. The band is 34 mm of satin alloy let into the wool, standing 1 mm proud, with the knob at its left. A hand reaching under the frame for the switch passes the band on the way.

Tradeoffs

The deepest of the three. Even splayed, the well narrows the useful viewing cone, so it wants hanging at eye height. It is also the most expensive, and the cost is the walnut.

Cheaper

Walnut-veneered plywood or stained poplar rails save about $37 and change the side view, not the front. Painting the bezel instead of buying alloy angle saves $12 and loses the one bright edge, which is the wrong economy.

Index's argument is that this should read as a piece of audio equipment and get there through seams and proportion rather than features. So the face is one sheet, edge to edge, with the wool field behind it and the picture let into a 12 mm graphite reveal.

The cheeks are after the Braun SK4, where a metal body with wooden side panels and a transparent lid was the entire material argument, and where the acrylic lid existed only because the sheet-metal one rattled. Soft Monument's chamfer becomes their 29 mm rake, so a 156 mm body reads thinner than it is. Console's band becomes the 40 mm alloy rail below the glass, carrying the knob on its face and the switch on its underside: one horizontal element for everything the hand does.

The first version used the project's usual 35 percent smoked grey. Over a lit picture that is right. Over a wool field the oatmeal rendered as pure black and the whole premise went with it.

Two things changed. The glazing is a light smoke, nearer picture glazing than sunglasses. And the field is chalk, a colourway none of the earlier palettes carried, because charcoal, oatmeal, oxblood, forest and ink all crush to black beside a 480 mm panel at full white.

Its best state is off

Every other object in this family is a duller version of itself when the music stops. Fascia is a pale wool mat under glass with a dark square in it. It looks like a frame, which is what Index was reaching for and could not get to while the front was a hole cut in a mat.

Tradeoffs

Lighter glazing shows more of the LED grid when off and gives the picture less contrast help. The 14 mm air gap costs about three pixels of parallax at 30 degrees. A full-face sheet is a mirror, so this is the design most sensitive to what stands opposite it.

This one is not a combination. It is the argument none of the six make.

A winged altarpiece has a weekday side and a feast-day side: shut through Lent and ordinary time, opened for feasts. Pacher's St Wolfgang has three states because it has two layers of wing. The Lenten veil, the German Hungertuch, is the same instinct with no picture at all, a cloth hung across the chancel to give what the sources call a fast of the eyes, and not removed until the Passion reading. Bang and Olufsen's Beovision Harmony is the living version: wood and aluminium covers that conceal the screen whenever it is off or only playing music.

Every other object here has an off state that is a dimmer version of the on state. This one has an off state that is a different object.

Closed, the album is behind laminated washi on an 80 mm kumiko grid, and what reaches the room is the record's colour without the record's picture. That is Noguchi's argument for Akari: light as the sculpture rather than light on a thing. Folded back, it is the picture, bare. Half open, it is both.

The paper is doing real work. A washi screen over an LED matrix is a diffuser, and diffusing is the traditional answer to exactly this problem. The rest of the project rejected opal acrylic on the grounds that the grid is the point, and that is still true. Here the diffusion is a state rather than a permanent filter. You choose which one you want by moving your hand.

The lattice knows where the seams are

Kumiko on an 80 mm grid, exactly half the 160 mm panel pitch, so bars land at 80 (both seams), 160 (mid panel) and 240 (the picture's edge), in both axes. The seams Nonet argued could never be hidden are, closed, under a bar.

The gesture is the control

Fukasawa's rule for the Muji player is that a thing on a wall should ask for one honest action, and it should be the action the form already suggests. You open it to look at it. Both meeting stiles carry a magnet and a reed switch behind the mat reads whether they are together. The mains switch stays under the bottom rail, because a door is not a disconnect.

The cost, stated plainly

Open, it claims 1320 mm of wall. Four bi-folding leaves were built first because they cut that to about 1000, and they were wrong: a folding pair needs its own stile on both sides of every joint, so four leaves put three 36 mm bands of solid walnut across a 640 mm face and the thing read as a window grille rather than a screen. Two leaves put one joint down the middle, which is what an altarpiece has anyway.

It is also the most joinery by a distance. A kumiko lattice is eighteen half-lapped pieces per leaf, and it is the only one of the three that a bad afternoon can ruin.

The one test that decides it

Hold one sheet of the real laminated washi in front of one lit panel. The renders say the closed state is a lantern. A sample sheet and ten minutes say whether it is a lantern or a dim grey rectangle, and that is the difference between a design and a nice drawing.

Money

Planning allowances, not quotes. The core build is about $150 with the smoked sheet, from the prices checked on 7 September. All three are above the $150 to $200 the earlier rounds targeted, and the reason is hardwood in all three and paper in one. That is a change of bracket, and it should be a decision rather than a drift.

Six faults the renders found

Worth writing down, because five of them were invisible in the numbers.

Mitre wingsThe rear taper pulled each rail's outer face in by 6 mm but not its length, so the 45 degree joint stopped closing and every corner grew a small tab. A tapered mitre has to shorten in two directions.

A louvre in the wellThe acrylic was 508 square inside a 496 throat, and the exposed 6 mm margin, seen at a grazing angle, showed a stack of total-internal-reflection ghosts of the LED grid. The bezel now laps its edge.

A second louvre, different causeThe wool's weave is two crossed wave bands at 3.85 mm, and face on that is right: sub-pixel, it averages into texture. Foreshortened onto the well's walls it aliased into nine soft flutes. Zeroing the relief did not fix it, because the same bands drive the colour as well. The lining is a flat tone with fine noise now.

The body standing past the frameThe old carcass ran its rails at the board's own outline, so from any oblique angle the black box stood past the walnut as a slab. The plinth is 46 mm in on every side now, which is the shadow gap the questionnaire asked for.

The field going blackFascia's entire premise failed at the project's usual tint. Both the glazing and the cloth had to change, and chalk is a new colourway because none of the existing five survives beside a lit panel.

Four leaves reading as a grilleThe bi-fold saved 320 mm of wall and cost the object its character. Two leaves, one joint.

What a render cannot settle

The paper is a translucent shader, not a measurement. The splay's benefit is geometry and holds. The wool's behaviour under a grazing halo does not, since the lining is now a shader twice over. Alcove's walnut is four mitres in hardwood at 22 mm, thin enough that a bad cut shows. And nobody has weighed any of these: the cleat rating and the anti-lift restraint are still physical work.
