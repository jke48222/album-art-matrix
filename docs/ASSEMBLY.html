What you are building

A shadow box. The nine panels bolt to a painted plywood backer, the backer drops into a rabbet cut in the back of a hardwood frame, and two sheets of acrylic sit in the same rabbet in front of the panels. The power supply and the Pi live in the cavity behind the backer, inside the frame, so one cord leaves the object.

Why this order

Eight parts are not here yet. Rather than stall, the sequence puts every job that needs only what is on the shelf first, and lets the waiting parts enter the build exactly where they are needed. The line across the middle is real: nothing above it is blocked.

The one honest cost of this order

Nine untested panels go on the backer in phase 4, before anything has been lit. That is the price of not waiting. Two things make it survivable, and both are built into the steps below: every panel is fastened so it can come off on its own without disturbing its neighbours, and you record which panel serial sits in which position. When phase 5 finds a dim panel or an odd brightness bin, you swap that one tile, not the wall.

Four things to sort out now, before they block a Saturday

The riser is a separate two dollar add-on on the bonnet's own product page. Order it today, or phase 5 stops the moment you open the box.
  • The VEML7700 has nowhere to plug in. It arrives with a STEMMA QT socket, which is Adafruit's little four pin snap connector, and the Triple Bonnet has no QT port.   • No USB audio input was ordered, only the microphone. Phase 6 has two halves and only the room-listening half is on its way.
  • Check whether the C14 inlets came with a glass fuse. That style takes a 5 x 20 mm cartridge fuse in a small drawer beside the switch, and they often ship empty. Bench and rules

Before any part comes out of a bag. This phase exists because the expensive mistakes on this build are the ones you find after nine panels are bolted down.

Space
1.2 m of clear bench, plus floor for a 480 mm dry layout
Tools
Strippers, ratcheting crimper, multimeter, iron, drivers, heat-shrink
Consumables
Masking tape, a fine Sharpie, zip ties, M3 assortment
Leaves you with
A labelled bench and three rules you will not break

The three rules

  1. Never hot-plug HUB75. HUB75 is the ribbon cable standard these panels use: sixteen wires carrying colour data, five row address lines, a clock and a latch. Hot-plugging means pushing that connector on or pulling it off while anything is powered. Do that and the panel's driver chips can take a voltage spike through a data pin, which kills them quietly, with no smoke and no obvious moment of failure. So: both supplies off, and the Pi off, before any ribbon moves.
  2. Panel power never passes through the Pi or the bonnet. The bonnet is the little board that sits on the Pi and turns its pins into three HUB75 sockets, and it carries data only. The panel-to-panel 5 V links built into these boards are rated around 4 to 6 amps, and nine panels want up to 36, so every panel gets its own fused pair of wires straight from the bus bar. There is no exception to this at nine panels, and none at one.
  3. Mains gets wired with the cord unplugged. Mains is the 120 volt side, everything from the wall socket up to the power supply's input terminals. No exposed copper outside a screw terminal, a firm pull on every conductor to prove it is tight, and the clear terminal cover back on before the cord goes anywhere near a socket.

Set the bench up

  1. Lay out three zones and keep them apart: mains at one end, DC and panels in the middle, Pi and data at the other end. Wires that cross should cross at right angles, not run alongside each other.
  2. Tape and label both ends of every cable you make, as you make it. A drop labelled P7 at one end and nothing at the other is not labelled.
  3. Keep every box, bag and bit of packing paper in the order it arrived until the wall is hung and working. The panels came from the UK with a return window, and the batch stickers on the boxes are the only same-batch evidence you will ever have.
  4. Put the multimeter somewhere you will actually reach for it. Every voltage claim in this document is a thing to measure, not a thing to assume.

Panel intake, no power

Ten panels, loose on a table, nothing plugged into anything. The full procedure is in scripts/PANEL-INTAKE.md. What follows is that procedure plus the two extra measurements this build needs, because phase 4 cannot start without them.

Needs
Ten panels, tape measure or calipers, phone camera, good light
Produces
qa/panel-NN.json and a filled qa/QA-SHEET.md
Also produces
The paper drilling template phase 4 depends on
  1. Inventory before anything else. Open all ten. Each box should hold a panel, a 30 cm 16-pin ribbon and a power lead. Note any box that is short, and check the parcel paperwork for a duty or VAT invoice.
  2. Photograph every batch and QC sticker before they scuff.
  3. Read the driver chip on every panel. The driver chips are the small black rectangles covering the back of the board, and they are what actually switch the LEDs on and off. Different families need different software handling, so the part number matters. This batch reads FM6124HJ, a conventional driver that needs no special start-up sequence, and all ten must match. A single odd panel out came from a different production batch, and in the middle of a 3 x 3 that reads as one visible tile.
  4. Measure one panel properly, spot check the rest.
    • Outline width and height. 160 mm each way is the expectation.
    • Depth from the panel face to the deepest thing on the back. This is the number the whole frame depth is derived from.
    • Mounting hole thread and positions.
    • Which edge the JIN and JOUT HUB75 headers sit on. Data enters at JIN and leaves at JOUT, so this decides ribbon routing and which way up each panel goes. The power header needs no checking, the spec puts it at the centre of the back.
  5. Decide the mount style now. Look at the back of a panel with a straight edge across it.
    • If the connectors and terminals sit in a recess, or stand less than about 4 mm proud of the mounting bosses, the panel can bolt flat to the backer and you only need slots for cable exit. Delete 12 mm from the depth budget.
    • If they stand proud, mount on 12 mm M3 standoffs. This is the safer default and the one the numbers in this document assume, because the gap doubles as a cooling plenum.
  6. Make the drilling template. Tape a sheet of paper over the back of one panel and push a scribe through each mounting hole. Trace the outline of the connector cluster and the power terminals. Write TOP on it. That single sheet is what phase 4 uses nine times, and it is far more accurate than measuring holes with a ruler.
  7. Butt two panels together and look at the seam. That gap is the gap the finished wall has, and it is what the diffuser in phase 9 has to hide.
  8. Lay out the full 3x3 on the floor at 480 mm square, put the Pi where it will live, and check a 30 cm ribbon actually reaches from the Pi to the first panel of each of the three rows, and from each panel to the next. If it does not reach, longer ribbons go in the same order as the bonnet, not a separate one later.
  9. Record it. Once per panel:
    python3 scripts/panel_qa.py intake --panel 1
    Read the resulting table down the columns rather than across the rows. A value that differs from the other nine is the finding.

Do not power a panel off something improvised

One 64 by 64 P2.5 panel pulls about 4 A at full white. A bench supply that sags, or a USB brick pressed into service, browns the panel out and teaches you nothing. Everything electrical waits for phase 5.

Pi and software check

The Pi is already flashed, bootstrapped and running both services. This phase is a check, not a build, and it exists so that phase 5 is a hardware question and only a hardware question. Full detail is in pi/PI-SETUP.md.

Needs
Pi 5 on its own 27 W USB-C supply, on the network
Produces
A wall that answers the network with no panel behind it
  1. Reach it. ssh wall. The alias in ~/.ssh/config is pinned to the Pi's address because album-matrix.local resolves inconsistently on this network. If it does not answer, Raspberry Pi Connect gives a shell without the LAN.
  2. Push current code. ./deploy.sh from the Mac. The Pi owns its own config.toml, which deploy seeds once and never overwrites, so calibration survives deploys.
  3. Confirm both services are enabled, not just running. Running means the program is alive right now. Enabled means the Pi will start it again by itself after a reboot. They are different states and only the second one survives a power cut:
    systemctl is-enabled album-art-renderer album-art-matrix
    Both must say enabled, or the wall comes back dark.
  4. Confirm the brain answers: curl -s http://wall:8788/state. You should get mode, brightness, rpm, effect, finish and the ticker fields back.
  5. Confirm the pipe exists and the renderer is holding it open. The pipe is a named FIFO, a file that behaves like a queue: one program writes pixel bytes into it and the other reads them out, and neither has to know anything about the other. It is what lets you restart the picture-making half without the panels going dark.
    ls -l /tmp/album-frame.fifo, then check the renderer log prints its mapped and dropped counts every 10 seconds.
  6. Two things still owed on the Pi, both needing your password:
    • The Wi-Fi powersave drop-in at /etc/NetworkManager/conf.d/wifi-powersave-off.conf. Not nmcli connection modify, because netplan regenerates the connection each boot.
    • Confirm the renderer unit is installed, not just present in the repo.
  7. Leave the panel geometry alone for now. Config stays at 64 by 64 and the renderer stays at one port, one chain. Phase 5 changes both at the same time, once there is something to look at.

Power system

One job: turn the 120 volts in the wall into a steady 5 volts sitting on a metal bar, with nine wires ready to leave from it. Nothing is connected to a panel, nothing lights up, and at the end you have measured a voltage rather than assumed one.

There are two electrical worlds here and they never mix. Mains is the 120 volt alternating current that comes out of a wall socket. It runs from the plug, through the switch and fuse on the frame, into the supply's input terminals, and stops there. It can kill you. The 5 volt side is everything after the supply. It cannot hurt you, but it can push 60 amps into a wire that was not built for it, which is why every panel gets its own fuse.

Build it in this order

  1. Learn the supply before you wire it. Take the LRS-350-5 out of the box and look along one long edge. Under a clear plastic cover is a row of screw terminals with a printed label above them. At one end, three terminals marked L, N and a ground symbol: that is the 120 volt input. At the other, several marked -V and +V: that is the 5 volt output. Also find a small brass screw near the output end, usually marked V ADJ or SVR. That is a potentiometer, a screw that changes a resistance, and it is how you set the exact output voltage. Nothing on the output side can hurt you. Everything on the input side can.
  2. Make a bench pigtail out of one power cord. A pigtail is just a short cable with bare wire on one end. Cut the rectangular socket end off one of the two computer power cords, the end that would plug into a PC, and keep the wall plug.
    You are stripping twice, not once. The cord has an outer jacket with three separately insulated wires running inside it.
    1. Take about 60 mm of the outer jacket off the cut end. Score it lightly all the way round with a knife and flex it until it parts, or use the jacket notch on the strippers. Go gently: cut too deep and you nick the insulation on the wires inside, which you will not see and which is the actual hazard here.
    2. Then strip about 8 mm off each of the three inner wires individually. Twist the strands of each one tight so nothing frays out sideways.
    • Black is Line, written L. This is the wire that carries the voltage.
    • White is Neutral, N. This is the return path.
    • Green is Earth. This is the safety wire. It bonds the supply's metal case to the ground pin in the wall, so that if a live wire ever touches the case the breaker opens instead of you completing the circuit. Never leave it off, and never put anything else on its terminal.
    You do not choose a gauge here. The pigtail is the cord, so it is whatever the cord already is, which for these Amazon Basics cords is 18 AWG. That is correct and nothing needs upgrading: the whole wall draws about 2.5 A at 120 V, and the cord is listed for far more. The 14 AWG in this build is for the 5 V side only, where the current is fourteen times higher.
    Land the three wires bare. Twisted stranded copper clamped under the Mean Well's screw plate is the normal, correct connection for this block. Do not crimp your blue ring terminals onto them: blue is sized for 16 to 14 AWG and 18 AWG rattles around inside the barrel, giving you a crimp that looks finished and is not. If you want terminals on the mains side, they need to be red, 22 to 18 AWG. And never tin the strands with solder before clamping them, because solder slowly creeps under pressure and the screw goes loose months later.
    Keep the second cord whole. That is the one that plugs into the frame's inlet.
  3. Land the three wires. The supply's input end has exactly three screws for them, labelled in print just above the terminals under the clear cover. One wire per screw, and every output screw stays empty.
    WireGoes toWhich is
    BlackLLine, sometimes printed AC/L. The wire carrying the voltage
    WhiteNNeutral, sometimes printed AC/N. The return
    Greenthe ground symbolEarth. Sometimes printed FG. It bonds the metal case
    Read the labels on your actual unit rather than counting positions from a photograph.
    Black and white are not interchangeable, even though a switching supply will run either way round. The switch and the fuse in the frame's inlet interrupt Line only, so if L and N are swapped, switching off leaves the supply's internals still connected to the live side of the circuit. It looks off and it is not.
    Push each wire in until its insulation almost touches the terminal, so no bare copper shows outside. Tighten, then pull firmly on each one. If a wire moves, it is not tight.
    Then, in this order: check nothing is on the output screws, look along the block for a stray whisker leaning toward its neighbour, put the clear cover back on, and only then plug into the wall and switch on.
    Set the meter up properly. Black lead in COM, red lead in the socket marked for volts. If your dial has numbered ranges rather than an AUTO position, choose 20 in the DCV group. The higher ranges work but cost you decimal places, and you are about to chase a difference of five hundredths of a volt.
    Black probe on any -V screw, red probe on any +V screw. It should read close to 5. A negative sign just means the probes are the other way round and is harmless. A reading of zero means no mains is arriving, so go back to the plug and the wiring, not to the supply.
  4. Trim it to 5.05 to 5.10 volts. Find the small brass screw near the output end, usually recessed and marked V ADJ or SVR. Leave the probes where they are so you can watch the number move, and turn the screw in small steps with a fine flat screwdriver. Clockwise normally raises the voltage, but do not take that on trust, make a quarter turn and read the meter. Some of these pots are multi-turn and travel slowly, others reach a stop quickly, so never force one past its end.
    Land it between 5.05 and 5.10 with nothing connected to the output. You aim slightly above 5 on purpose: wire loses a little voltage along its length, so 5.07 at the supply arrives as roughly 5.00 at the furthest panel, and panels start misbehaving below about 4.80.
    Then switch off, unplug, and give it a minute before touching the input side again. The supply holds a charge for a few seconds after the plug comes out.
  5. Know the colour trap before you touch the inlet. The cord and the inlet do not use the same colour convention, and they disagree on the most dangerous wire.
    JobOn the US cordOn the Antrader inlet
    Lineblackred
    Neutralwhiteblack
    Earthgreenyellow and green
    Black means Line on the cord and Neutral on the inlet. Wire by colour habit here and you swap Line and Neutral without noticing. Wire by job, and confirm the job with the test below rather than with the label.
  6. Identify the inlet's three connections. The C14 inlet is the socket that ends up mounted in the frame: a panel-mount socket with a rocker switch and a fuse drawer built in, so one switch kills the whole wall. On the back are three connections, either bare spade tabs or leads already crimped on.
    Do not guess which is which. The tab layout varies between modules and the printing is often absent or wrong. There is a test that settles it, and it needs nothing plugged in anywhere. Meter on continuity, the setting that beeps, one probe on a pin in the socket face and the other on a tab or lead, and work through every combination.
    RoundSet it like thisWhat it tells you
    1Fuse in, switch ONAll three paths beep. This is your map of which pin reaches which tab
    2Switch OFFWhatever still beeps is Earth. Earth is never switched, on any module
    3Switch ON, fuse pulled outWhichever path stops beeping is Line. The fuse is always in the line side
    Whatever is left over is Neutral. Put a tape label on each lead the moment you identify it, because you will not remember five minutes later. On this module the answer should come out as red for Line, black for Neutral, and the yellow and green one for Earth. If your test disagrees with that, trust the test, because it is measuring your actual part.
    The short red and black loops on the back are factory jumpers that carry Line through the fuse and then the switch. Leave them exactly as they are.
    Opening the fuse drawer. Unplug everything first. The drawer is the small panel on the front between the C14 socket and the rocker switch, and it pulls straight out toward you. Run a fingernail around its edges to find the release: on most of these it is a shallow notch on one edge that takes the tip of a small flat screwdriver, and on some it is a tab you press while pulling. Lever gently and pull square. Do not twist it, and do not force it, because these are stiff from moulding flash rather than latched.
    Inside is a plastic carrier holding a 5 x 20 mm glass fuse, and often a second bay beside it. Only one bay is in circuit, the other is storage for a spare, so if you end up fitting a fuse and still get nothing, try the other slot before you suspect anything else.
    If the drawer is empty you need a 5 A, 250 V, slow blow. And if you cannot get the drawer open at all, round 1 above has already told you what you need: if the line path beeps with the switch on, there is a good fuse in there.
  7. Land the inlet on the supply. Unplug first, then take the pigtail's three wires back out of the terminals. Keep that pigtail, label it, and set it aside: it is what feeds the LRS-50-5 at first light in phase 5. If you would rather not have a cut cord end on the bench at all, wire the spare inlet to the LRS-50-5 instead and keep both cords whole.
    Then the inlet's three long leads go into the same three terminals: red to L, black to N, yellow and green to the ground symbol. Same clamp, same tug test.
    The earth lead comes with a ring terminal crimped on the far end. That is made for a stud, a threaded post you slide the ring over and hold down with a nut. The supply does not have one. It has clamp terminals, the kind in the drawing above, where the screw drives a plate down onto bare copper and never touches the wire itself. A ring will not sit properly under that plate.
    So cut the ring off, strip about 8 mm, and land it exactly the way you landed the pigtail: screw out, wire in under the plate, screw down, tug. Do the same to anything on the ends of the red and black leads. This block wants bare copper, not a connector.
    The inlet is not mounted in anything yet, because the frame does not exist until phase 10, so it will be sitting loose on the bench with mains on it. If yours has bare spade tabs rather than pre-crimped leads, cover every one before the cord goes near a socket: fully insulated female spades, 6.3 mm, or a crimp with heat shrink pulled down over it. Your terminal kit is rings only, so this may be one more thing to order.
  8. Run L straight through for now. The inlet's switched output goes directly to the supply's L terminal. The inrush limiter belongs in that line and has not arrived, so leave a little slack in the wire: phase 8 cuts it, splices the limiter in, and needs the room.
  9. Prove the earth first, with nothing plugged in. This is the one check that confirms the safety wire actually does its job end to end, and it takes ten seconds.
    Meter on continuity. Put one probe on the round earth pin of the wall plug at the far end of the cord, with the cord plugged into the inlet but not into the wall. Put the other probe on the metal case of the power supply, anywhere bare. It should beep.
    If it does not, the earth path is broken somewhere between the plug and the case, and that is the fault you least want to discover later, because nothing else in the build will ever hint at it.
  10. Prove the inlet path, both ways. Put the fuse back in the drawer if you pulled it out. Cover back on the supply, every bare tab insulated, and put the loose inlet somewhere it cannot be knocked or touch anything metal. Plug the whole cord into the inlet and the inlet into the wall.
    • Switch ON. Meter on the output should read the same number you trimmed to. A lower reading means a terminal is not tight. Nothing at all means the fuse or the switch.
    • Switch OFF. The reading should collapse to zero within a second or two as the output capacitors drain.
    The second half is the test that matters. If the voltage does not fall when you switch off, the switch is sitting in the neutral instead of the line, which means your L and N leads are swapped. Everything works, the wall lights, and the supply stays connected to the live side of the circuit whenever it is "off". Swap the two leads and test again.
    Once both halves pass, the mains side of this build is finished until the inrush limiter goes in at phase 8. Everything from here on is the 5 V side, where a mistake costs you a fuse rather than anything worse.
  11. Insulate everything you touched. Heat shrink is a plastic sleeve that pulls tight when you warm it, and you now own a heat gun for exactly this. Slide a piece onto the wire before you make each joint, not after, then warm it until it grips. Clear cover back on the supply, no bare copper anywhere outside a screw terminal. Then look at the whole mains run under a bright light and ask one question: could a finger reach anything live?
  12. Set out the two bus bars. A bus bar is a solid metal strip in a plastic housing, with a row of terminal screws along it and a snap-on cover. It exists so that a dozen wires can share one electrical point, because stacking a dozen ring terminals onto the supply's own screws neither fits nor holds.
    Yours came as a proper pair, red for positive and black for negative, so they are already marked and there is nothing to label. Each has two kinds of connection, and the difference decides your whole layout:
    • Twelve small screws in two rows. These are the branch connections and they are what almost everything lands on. Count yours before planning.
    • One large 1/4 in stud at the end, meant for a heavy feed cable. The two chunky copper lugs in the bag are for it, sized for 2 AWG, which is far larger than anything in this build.
    Do not screw them down yet. The backer they live on does not exist until phase 4. Lay them where they will sit, side by side and a short run from the supply, and wire them there.
  13. The layout lands exactly on twelve. Three trunk feeds from the supply, plus nine panel drops, is twelve connections per bar, which is what the small screws give you.
    ConnectionRed barBlack bar
    Three trunk feeds from the supply3 screws3 screws
    Nine panel drops9 screws9 screws
    Large 1/4 in studunused, boot onstar ground to the Pi
    Spread the three trunk feeds along the bar rather than clustering them, so current enters at several points instead of running the strip's whole length. Both ends and the middle is fine.
    The black bar's stud is the natural star ground point, the single place every return meets, so that is where the Pi's ground strap lands in phase 4. Make that strap from 14 AWG rather than something thinner, so a 1/4 in ring crimps properly onto it. Then the rubber boots go over both studs, the used one and the unused one, because a bare stud beside a live bar is exactly what the boots are for.
  14. Use the right rings for the right hole. Two sizes, two jobs, and mixing them up wastes an afternoon.
    • The twenty four blue rings that came with the bars fit the twelve small screws. That is twelve per bar, exactly enough, and blue is the correct band for 14 AWG. Use these.
    • The 1/4 in rings from the ALBO kit are for the large stud only.
    • The 2 AWG copper lugs are not used in this build. Put them back in the bag.
    • The four pointed self-tappers are for mounting the bars in phase 4. Keep them with the bars.
    Every trunk and drop wire is terminated differently at each end, which is the easy thing to half-do: bare copper into a clamp terminal at the supply or panel end, a ring terminal under a screw at the bus bar end.
  15. Every wire has two different ends. This is the thing that is easy to half-do, so it is worth saying once, plainly, because it holds for the trunks now and the drops later.
    EndWhat it lands inHow it is finished
    Bus bara small screw threaded into the metal stripBlue ring terminal, crimped, screw through it
    Supplya clamp terminal, same as the mains sideBare copper, twisted, no connector at all

    Strip about 8 mm at each end. At the ring end that is roughly the length of the ring's metal barrel, so hold one against the wire and match it.
  16. Cut three lengths of pair at 300 mm. That is the answer for the trunks. Three pieces, split at the ends, giving six wires.

  26. Crimp properly. Crimping means squeezing a metal barrel onto stripped wire hard enough that the two become one piece. Done right it outlasts solder and survives vibration.
    1. Match the die to the colour. Your crimper has three positions marked by wire range or by colour. Red is 22 to 18 AWG, blue is 16 to 14, yellow is 12 to 10. Everything in this build is 14 AWG in a blue terminal, so it is the blue jaw every time. The wrong die either fails to grip or crushes the sleeve.
      Only the barrel enters the jaw. On a ring terminal the loop hangs outside the tool. If the jaws will not close, rotate the terminal ninety degrees so the ring points away from them.
    2. Strip the length of the barrel. Hold a terminal against the wire and match it, usually 6 to 8 mm. Too short and the copper does not fill the tube. Too long and bare wire pokes out the back.
    3. Push the wire in until it stops, then look at the ring end. The copper should just reach the far end of the barrel.
    4. Put the barrel in the die, not the ring and not the back edge where the sleeve meets the wire. The jaw wants the middle of the metal tube.
    5. Squeeze until the ratchet lets go. That is what the ratchet is for: it will not release until the crimp is fully formed, so you cannot under-squeeze by accident. If you need to abort, there is a release lever.
    6. Pull hard on the wire. A good crimp does not move. If it slides at all, cut it off and start again. A crimp that pulls out on the bench will also pull out on the wall.
    7. Heat the sleeve, but only if it is that kind. Hold one terminal from each bag side by side. Heat shrink has a longer sleeve running back over the wire past the metal barrel, often slightly translucent, and feels waxy. Plain nylon has a short, hard, opaque collar about as long as the barrel. The ALBO kit is heat shrink by its listing, so whichever looks shorter and harder is the plain sort.
      Heat shrink: warm it until the sleeve grips the wire's insulation, moving the gun rather than holding it still. Plain nylon: nothing to do.
      If you cannot tell, do not heat it. Heating a nylon terminal deforms and weakens it, while skipping heat on a heat shrink one costs nothing that matters indoors in a wooden frame. The crimp is what holds the wire either way.
    Never solder a crimp. Solder wicks up inside the strands and makes the wire rigid exactly where it flexes, and it snaps there months later.

Meter discipline

The multimeter in this build is a ten dollar meter, and cheap meters are cheap partly because their probes and internals are not rated to survive a mistake at mains voltage. It is perfectly good for what this phase asks of it: DC volts on the 5 V side, and continuity with everything unplugged.

5d. White balance, the step that decides how it looks

Tell a HUB75 panel to show white and it does not show white. The red, green and blue LEDs in each pixel are not equally efficient, and the green and blue ones win, so full white comes out as a pale cyan. The fix is a gain per channel, a number the software multiplies each colour by before sending it, so that turning all three fully on actually looks white. Typical values are around R 1.00, G 0.75, B 0.55, meaning green is knocked back to three quarters and blue to just over half.
The values sitting in config.toml today are labelled a guess, because nobody has measured these panels. Skipping this step is the single reason most LED matrix art on the internet looks cyan. Full procedure is scripts/WB-PROCEDURE.md.

  1. Solder the header strip to the TCS34725 breakout. Wire it to the spare Pico 2 W: VIN to 3V3 OUT on pin 36, GND to pin 38, SDA to GP4 on pin 6, SCL to GP5 on pin 7.
  2. Flash MicroPython, then mpremote cp scripts/pico_colorimeter.py :main.py && mpremote repl.
  3. Warm the panels up for ten minutes at working brightness first. LEDs get dimmer and shift colour slightly as they heat up, and they reach a steady temperature after a few minutes. Measuring cold gives you numbers for a state the wall is almost never in.
  4. python scripts/show_white.py --sink pi for full white with no white balance applied.
  5. Hold the sensor flat against the panel face, shaded from room light. The REPL streams suggested gains continuously. Take readings at the centre and at two corners.
  6. Put the settled numbers into [whitebalance], restart the brain, and compare the wall against the same cover on your phone. That comparison is the deliverable, not the numbers.
  7. No sensor to hand? Show a white heavy sleeve, hold the phone next to the panel with the same cover, and nudge g down until the white stops looking minty, then b until it stops looking icy. Start from 1.00, 0.75, 0.55.
You will do this twice. These gains are measured against bare panels. The acrylic in phase 9 has its own colour cast, and phase 9 sends you back here to re-measure through the finished stack. Measuring now is still worth it, because it separates a panel problem from an acrylic problem.

audio in + mic

Ears: line-in and microphone

Two USB devices and some ALSA configuration. This is the hardware half of the path that lets the wall identify a record by listening to it, rather than by asking an API.

Waiting on
USB audio input adapter, and a mini USB microphone
Produces
Two verified capture devices on the Pi

Be clear about what this buys you today

There is no fingerprinting code in the repository. brain/nowplaying/__init__.py reserves the name acoustid and brain/main.py prints a message saying that adapter is skipped. Separately, tempo is already solved elsewhere: Tessera measures BPM from the phone's own microphone with a spectral flux onset detector and a comb filter, so the wall does not need the Pi to hear anything in order to spin in time.

So install these, prove they capture clean audio, and label them. The payoff arrives when the Chromaprint to AcoustID to MusicBrainz to Cover Art Archive adapter is written, and that is a software job, not this one.

Steps

  1. Check which input you actually have. There are two very different signal levels with the same shaped plug. A microphone level input expects a few millivolts, because that is all a microphone produces. A line level input expects around a volt, which is what an amplifier or a stereo puts out. Feed a line level signal into a microphone input and it clips into distortion long before the software ever sees it.
    The cheap USB audio dongles are microphone input only. The Behringer UCA202 has real RCA line inputs and is the right part for a feed from the amplifier. Neither is ordered yet, so this phase currently has only the mic half of the job.
  2. Plug in and list what Linux found. arecord -l asks ALSA, the Linux sound layer, to list every capture device it can see. Write down the card and device numbers it prints, and note that those numbers can shuffle across reboots, so anything you automate later should refer to a device by name rather than by number.
  3. Record ten seconds from each and listen back:
    arecord -D plughw:CARD=<name> -f cd -d 10 /tmp/test.wav
  4. Set the levels with alsamixer -c <card>. Aim for the loudest moments to land around 12 dB below the maximum, which leaves headroom for a sudden peak. A recording that is too quiet can be turned up later. A recording that clipped has had its peaks flattened off and the shape is gone for good, and both fingerprinting and beat detection care about shape rather than loudness.
  5. Route the line feed. Take it from the amplifier's tape or record output if it has one, which is fixed level and independent of the volume knob. A headphone splitter works and is level dependent, so the fingerprint quality then moves with the volume.
  6. Place the microphone at the frame's lower edge, facing into the room, away from the panels. It hears the room, so it also hears you. That is the point, and it is worth deciding now whether you want a live microphone on your wall.
  7. Strain relieve both cables at the Pi. A USB device that intermittently re-enumerates is the worst kind of fault to chase later.

VEML7700

Eye: ambient light sensor

A three dollar part that decides whether the wall is a piece or a nuisance. A fixed brightness that reads well at noon is blinding at eleven at night.

Waiting on
VEML7700 breakout, and a QT to female jumper cable
Produces
A sensor the Pi can read, mounted where it sees the room

Two things to know before you wire it

  • The Triple Bonnet has no STEMMA QT port. That is why the parts list carries the QT to female jumper cable. Without it there is nothing to plug the breakout into.
  • There is no auto-brightness code. Nothing in the repository mentions this part. What this phase gives you is a sensor that reads correctly and is mounted correctly, so that writing the loop later is twenty lines against brain/control.py's existing brightness field rather than a hardware session.

Steps

  1. Find free I2C pins. I2C is a two wire bus: one clock line, one data line, and any number of chips hanging off the pair, each answering to its own numeric address. On a Pi it lives on GPIO 2 and GPIO 3, which are physical pins 3 and 5 on the header.
    The bonnet sits on that header and takes most of it. Before wiring anything, check which pins the Triple Bonnet actually claims and whether 3 and 5 survive. If they do, that is the sensor's home.
  2. If they are not free, put the VEML7700 on the spare Pico 2 W, which is already the host for the colorimeter, and let it report over USB serial. It is one more USB cable and it removes the pin conflict entirely.
  3. Wire it. VIN to 3V3 on pin 1, GND to pin 6, SDA to pin 3, SCL to pin 5.
  4. Prove it. i2cdetect -y 1 scans the bus and prints a grid of every address that answered. The VEML7700 answers at 0x10. If nothing appears, the wiring is wrong or the sensor has no power, and no amount of software will find it.
    Then read an actual value, cover the sensor with your hand, and read it again. If the number does not move, it is powered and talking but not seeing the room.
  5. Mount it where it sees the room and not the wall. The right place is a 5 mm hole in the underside of the bottom frame rail, sensor behind it, pointing down and out. If it can see the panels, either directly or via a reflection off a nearby surface, the loop will chase its own tail: brighter wall, brighter reading, brighter wall.
  6. Do this before the frame is glued up if you can. Drilling a 5 mm hole in a finished hardwood rail is a worse afternoon than drilling it in a loose one, so mark it in phase 10 while the rails are still separate.

SL22 10005

Inrush limiter

One small black disc spliced into the mains hot line. Nine panels total roughly 15,000 uF of bulk capacitance, and a 60 A supply switching into that draws a very large, very short current spike at the instant the switch closes. The limiter is 10 ohm when cold, which turns that spike into a soft rise, and it drops to almost nothing once it warms through. It was bought as insurance rather than as a requirement, which is exactly why it can wait until here.

Waiting on
SL22 10005 inrush limiter, 10 ohm cold, 5 A steady
Undoes
The straight L link left in place at the end of phase 3
Produces
A wall that comes up quietly instead of with a thump

Do this while the backer is still loose

The supply lives in the frame cavity from phase 10 onward, and its terminal block ends up facing a wall. Splicing a mains component in there afterwards is a bad afternoon. Fit it now, on the bench, with the board flat and the terminal block facing you.

Steps

  1. Kill it properly. Switch off at the inlet, pull the cord out of the wall socket, and give it a minute. Only then take the terminal cover off.
  2. Break the L link. Phase 3 ran the inlet's switched output straight to the supply's L terminal. Unscrew that wire at the supply end and shorten it enough to leave room for the limiter and two joints. Neutral and earth are not touched.
  3. Splice the limiter in series on that hot line, one lead to the wire from the inlet and one going on to the supply's L terminal. Crimped butt splices, heat shrink over each joint, no bare metal.
  4. Give it air. The SL22 runs genuinely hot in normal operation, well past anything you want to hold. It must not rest against wire insulation, the plywood backer, a cable tie, or the supply's own case. Let its own leads hold it in free space, or stand it off on an insulating mount.
  5. Tug every joint, put the terminal cover back, and look at the whole mains run once more with the light on.
  6. Prove it. Plug in with all nine panels connected and switch on at the inlet. Before the limiter there is a hard thump and sometimes a flicker on the house breaker. After it, the wall should come up quietly. That difference, heard once, is the whole test.
One limiter covers both supplies if you ever run the LRS-50-5 bench supply off the same inlet. Together they draw around 4 A at 120 V, and the part is rated 5 A steady.

3 hours
both acrylics

Optical stack

Two sheets of acrylic in front of the LEDs, each doing a different job.
Opal acrylic is white plastic with the diffusing agent mixed through the material rather than printed on the surface, so light entering anywhere leaves spread out. That is what turns 4,096 separate dots into a picture.
The second sheet is ND, short for neutral density, which means it darkens everything by the same amount without tinting it. "LED grey" is the smoked version sold for exactly this. Room light has to pass through it, bounce off the panel, and pass through it again, so it is attenuated twice on the way back and only once on the way out. Costing yourself some brightness buys you a great deal of contrast, and it is the whole difference between an object that emits light and an object that is a switched-off screen.

Waiting on
Opal diffuser acrylic, and LED-grey smoked acrylic at 3 mm
Needs the wall lit
The trial below is impossible before phase 5
Produces
Two sheets cut to 496 mm square, and a re-checked power budget

9a. Run the trial before you cut anything

  1. Get offcuts or sample squares of each candidate: 3 mm opal, the 6.35 mm translucent white, and the 3 mm LED grey. Plastics shops keep a scrap bin and will usually hand you pieces.
  2. Put a real album cover on the wall, one with fine type on the sleeve, not a test pattern. Type is the thing that dies first when a diffuser is too thick.
  3. Hold each sample over one panel at 0, 3, 6 and 10 mm of standoff. Use stacked coins or washers as spacers. Look from two metres, which is the real viewing distance, not from 300 mm.
  4. Then add the grey sheet in front and look again, both with the room lit and with it dark. The grey costs you brightness once on the way out and twice on anything reflecting back, which is where the contrast comes from.
  5. Pick, then commit. The default recommendation is 3 mm opal at a 3 mm standoff with 3 mm LED grey in front. If the 6.35 mm sheet looks right to you at zero standoff, take it, and correct the depth budget by the difference.

9b. Cut and prepare

  1. Have the shop cut both sheets to 496 x 496 mm. A 500 mm cut in acrylic by hand goes wrong in ways that are visible from across a room. Ask them to leave the protective masking on both faces.
  2. Do not drill the acrylic. Nothing in this design needs a hole. The rabbet holds both sheets by their edges, which is also what lets them expand without cracking.
  3. Leave 1 mm of clearance a side inside the 498 mm groove. Acrylic expands and contracts with temperature far more than wood does, roughly a millimetre over half a metre across a normal room's temperature swing. A sheet cut to a perfect fit has nowhere to go when it grows, so it bows forward instead.
  4. Make the spacers. Nine or twelve small pieces of 3 mm material around the perimeter, between the panel faces and the diffuser, so the gap is held evenly. Adhesive felt or cork works and it also stops any rattle.
  5. Peel the masking last, at fit-up in phase 10, and handle the sheets by their edges from then on.

9c. Re-check two things the acrylic changed

  1. Brightness and current. Diffuser and grey together pass roughly a fifth of the light. You will want to raise the renderer's -b cap to compensate, and that raises current. Re-measure at the bus bar with a bright frame up, and confirm you still have supply headroom before you settle on a value.
  2. White balance. The stack has its own colour cast. Redo the phase 5 measurement with the sensor against the finished front surface rather than the bare panel, and keep both sets of gains in the config comments so you know which is which.

Commissioning

The wall works on the bench. This phase is about whether it still works in two hours, and after a power cut, and when nobody is watching it.

Thermal soak

  1. Run normal content at final brightness for two hours, fully assembled. Album art is a low duty cycle load, meaning most LEDs are off most of the time and the average power is far below the full white figure. But the panels are now sealed behind two sheets of acrylic, which is a different thermal problem from a bare panel on an open bench, and two hours is roughly how long a box this size takes to settle at a steady temperature.
  2. After two hours, put the back of your hand on the backer above the top vent row. Warm is expected. Uncomfortable to hold means the vents are not doing enough, and the fix is more holes, not less brightness.
  3. Check the supply's own case temperature and that nothing is resting against the NTC.
  4. Look at a flat mid grey field and look for any brightness gradient from bottom to top. A wall that is hotter at the top will show it there first.

Survives a reboot

  1. sudo systemctl enable --now album-art-renderer album-art-matrix. Both, or a reboot leaves the panels dark.
  2. Pull the power at the wall switch, wait ten seconds, switch back on. Time how long until the wall shows something. Everything should come back with no keyboard involved.
  3. Confirm the Wi-Fi powersave drop-in is in place so the Pi does not silently drop off the network overnight.
  4. Check the wall answers Tessera from the phone, and that the widget updates.
